Racing and competition

The Boston Half on Sunday the eighth produced the first sub-hour half marathon run in Massachusetts. Habtom Samuel of Eritrea broke away shortly after fifteen kilometres and ran the last four miles alone to finish in fifty-nine minutes forty-nine seconds, according to the Boston Athletic Association. That took forty-five seconds off the event record of one hour, thirty-four seconds, set thirteen years ago by Lelisa Desisa. Andrea Kiptoo was second in one hour, one minute and three seconds, Abel Kipchumba third in one hour, one minute and sixteen. Jack Aho was sixth and top American in one hour, one minute and fifty-three seconds, which the B A A lists as the fastest by an American in the event's history. Samuel said afterwards that he knew at ten kilometres they were on for the record.

Fentaye Belayneh of Ethiopia became the first woman to win the Boston Half three years running. She went through five kilometres in sixteen thirty-eight and ten in thirty-three oh two, sat in the pack over the rolling course, and outsprinted Winfred Mbithe by two seconds, one hour, nine minutes and fourteen seconds to one hour, nine minutes and sixteen. Daniel Romanchuk set an event record in the men's wheelchair race.

In triathlon, Magnus Ditlev of Denmark won Ironman Florida in Panama City Beach on Saturday the seventh in seven hours, nineteen minutes and seventeen seconds. Slowtwitch's report gives his splits as forty-seven fifty-two for the swim, three hours, fifty-one minutes and sixteen seconds on the bike, and two hours, thirty-three minutes and thirty-one seconds for the marathon, and says the time is the fastest ever at an Ironman-branded race, ahead of Kristian Blummenfelt's seven twenty-one twelve at Cozumel, and quicker than the seven twenty-one oh four Sam Laidlow set at Challenge Roth this year. The bike split is also reported as a full-distance best. Rudy Von Berg was second and Matt Hanson third, with a two hours, twenty-eight minute marathon. Alanis Siffert of Switzerland won the women's race. A caution, which is my own: Slowtwitch's headline calls this a world record, but long-course triathlon has no ratified records. Courses, currents and conditions differ, so a world best is a fair description and a world record is not.

World Athletics ratified four world records on Monday the ninth. Two are in our scope. Josh Kerr's mile of three minutes, forty-two point six six seconds in London on the eighteenth of July, which took the record from Hicham El Guerrouj's three forty-three point one three, set in Rome in nineteen ninety-nine. World Athletics gives Kerr's splits as fifty-five point three at four hundred metres, one fifty-one point one at eight hundred, and three twenty-seven point six two at fifteen hundred, faster than his own British record for that distance. And Yomif Kejelcha's half marathon of fifty-six minutes and fifty-one seconds in Buenos Aires on the twenty-third of August, twenty-nine seconds under Jacob Kiplimo's previous record. His second ten kilometres took twenty-six minutes and forty-five seconds. The other two are Alison dos Santos's forty-five point eight oh in the four hundred metres hurdles and Masai Russell's twelve point oh nine in the hundred metres hurdles, both in Zurich.

A day later, World Athletics published the qualification system for the Los Angeles Olympics in twenty twenty-eight. The marathon entry standards are two hours and five minutes for men and two hours, twenty-two minutes and twenty seconds for women. The marathon window opened on the first of November and runs to the end of April twenty twenty-eight. The split between the two routes in has changed: about forty percent of athletes are expected to qualify by standard and sixty percent through world rankings, where it used to be half and half. In the marathon, anyone ranked above fifty-sixth on the Road to L A list on the nineteenth of March twenty twenty-eight, at three per country, is in, and a top-five finish at a Platinum Label marathon counts as the standard. World Athletics explains the fast marathon standards with a line worth quoting: "in an Olympic year, times are between thirty to sixty seconds faster than the trends would forecast." A national Olympic committee can still reallocate a place to an athlete who has run two hours eleven, or two hours twenty-nine for women, inside the window.

Doping and governance

Kenya's anti-doping agency, ADAK, has provisionally suspended Mercy Oketch, the national four hundred metres record holder indoors and out. Marathon Handbook, reading ADAK's list, reports two substances: trimetazidine, a heart drug prescribed for angina that WADA classes as a metabolic modulator, and furosemide, a diuretic on the list as a masking agent. The entry is dated the twenty-sixth of October. The list gives no test date and does not say whether the sample was taken in or out of competition. Oketch is twenty-four, set her outdoor record of fifty point one four in Nairobi in May twenty twenty-five, and was fifth in the World Indoor final in March, the first Kenyan woman to reach it. As with every case on this programme, a provisional suspension is not a finding. Trimetazidine is a non-specified substance, which under World Athletics rules makes the suspension mandatory while the case is heard.

The same agency issued a correction that deserves more attention than it will get. On the fifth of November ADAK said the athlete suspended from the eighth of October for the glucocorticoid triamcinolone acetonide is Cherokony Noah Kibet, who races three thousand metres and longer. It is not Noah Kibet, the eight hundred metres runner and twenty twenty-two World Indoor silver medallist, whose name and photograph had circulated with the story. ADAK asked outlets to correct their reports. The cost of a wrong name match falls on an athlete who did nothing, which is why the correction deserves the same reach as the original reports.

The German broadcaster A R D published on Sunday the eighth the headline figure from a documentary series on child doping: at least ten million minors worldwide affected. The method is worth understanding before the number is repeated. A R D took the United Nations range of a hundred and ninety-five to two hundred and sixty million children aged eight to seventeen in competitive sport, used about two hundred million, and applied the average from forty-four anonymous surveys in fifteen countries, which put use of hard substances such as anabolic steroids and growth hormone at five to six percent. Paulo David, formerly secretary of the United Nations Committee on the Rights of the Child, told them: "there is a big, massive problem, and nothing is being done about it." Against that, WADA's own Operation Refuge report found one thousand four hundred and sixteen minors testing positive worldwide between twenty twelve and September twenty twenty-three, about a hundred and eighteen a year. A R D itself says an exact figure cannot be determined and that the surveys differ in method, size and timing. My reading: the ten million is an extrapolation from self-report in fifteen countries to the whole world, so it is an order of magnitude, not a count. The more robust point is the gap between survey prevalence of several percent and about a hundred positive tests a year worldwide, and it does not depend on the extrapolation. The series is in the A R D Media Library from the thirteenth.

General health and fitness news

The largest piece of new evidence this week is a randomised trial in Nature Medicine, published on Monday the ninth, by Daniel Green, Bu Yeap and colleagues in Western Australia. A hundred and twenty adults with overweight or obesity were all treated with tirzepatide for forty weeks, and half were randomised to supervised resistance training three times a week. Adherence to the training was ninety-one percent. Weight loss was the same in both arms, about twenty-two and a half kilograms. Fat loss was the same. What differed was lean soft tissue: four point six seven kilograms lost on the drug alone against three point five seven with training, a difference of about one point one kilograms, with a confidence interval of zero point four to one point seven five. Strength diverged in direction, not just size. On the drug alone, bench press fell by about four kilograms, lat pulldown by four and a half and knee extension by four and a half. With training, they rose by four point seven, eight point six and five point nine kilograms. The authors' own caveats: participants were middle-aged to older, initially sedentary, and without diabetes or cardiovascular disease, so the result may not generalise to everyone on the drug, and lean soft tissue by D X A is not the same as skeletal muscle. The finding that matters to anyone who lifts is the strength result. By my arithmetic, lean mass loss was cut by only about a quarter, but strength went up instead of down.

A cohort study in Circulation, Population Health and Outcomes, published on the second of November, used the All of Us programme's linked Fitbit and health records for ten thousand three hundred and sixty-two American adults, median age fifty-three, seventy percent women, with at least ninety valid nights over a hundred and eighty days. Over a median of three point seven years, two hundred and thirty developed the composite of heart attack, heart failure, cardiomyopathy or stroke. Each extra twenty-nine minutes of night-to-night variability in sleep duration, one standard deviation, was associated with a hazard ratio of one point two four, confidence interval one point oh nine to one point four one. Variability in bedtime and wake time showed much the same. Mean sleep duration was not associated, at zero point nine six. This is observational, the event count is modest, and Fitbit staging is an estimate. The authors call their one novel finding, that more sleep cycles go with less atrial arrhythmia, one requiring confirmation. The plain reading is that regularity carried more signal than average duration in this sample.

The Santa Barbara Half Marathon on Sunday the eighth ended with thirty of seven thousand five hundred registered runners treated for symptoms consistent with heat illness, according to the county health department as reported by Edhat. Twenty-six went to emergency departments, four were treated at the scene, and three remained in hospital on Monday. The first call came at eight forty-one in the morning. Inspectors who had checked the race's electrolyte drink preparation on the Friday and Saturday found, in the county's words, "No findings". The county health officer, Henning Ansorg, said the incident "appears to be an isolated event connected directly to race participation and the heat". Police are investigating, and there has been social media speculation about a water station that officials have not supported. The general lesson, which is mine and not the county's, is old: an unseasonably warm race day catches out people who trained in cool weather, and a later finish means more of the race in the heat.

Training intensity and the zone two argument

The running science this week was dominated by an argument rather than a paper. On The Real Science of Sport, the show notes summarise it as Peter Attia admitting that zone two is not needed for people training a couple of hours a week. I have not read or heard Attia's own words on this, so I will report the reaction, not characterise his position.

The strongest version of the case against zone two as a public-health prescription is a narrative review published in Sports Medicine in twenty twenty-five, by Kristi Storoschuk, Martin Gibala, Brendon Gurd and a colleague, titled "Much Ado About Zone Two". They argue that the popular recommendation rests largely on observational data from elite endurance athletes who do a lot of easy work and have high mitochondrial capacity, and they conclude that current evidence does not support zone two as the optimal intensity for improving mitochondrial or fat oxidative capacity. They add that higher intensities matter more for cardiometabolic health, particularly when total training volume is low. That last condition is the important one. Their target is the person training a few hours a week, not the runner doing eight hours.

Steve Magness answered on Monday the ninth with a post titled "We Settled the Zone Two versus H I I T Debate in the Nineteen Fifties". His argument has three parts. First, that zones are rough categories; in his words, zone two is "a very rough idea to categorize 'pretty easy running.'" Second, that most training studies last six to ten weeks, and intensity works quickly while an aerobic base takes time, so short studies will tend to favour intensity. Third, that studies measure V O two max, economy and threshold when fresh, but not how well those hold up late in a long race. He criticises a twenty twenty-five review, which he does not name, for focusing narrowly on mitochondria, and my inference is that he means the Storoschuk paper. His summary of what has survived a century of coaching is: "Lots of easy, some moderate, a bit of hard to sharpen up, and occasionally go see God to realign perspectives."

These two positions are closer than the noise suggests. Storoschuk and colleagues are talking about the general public at low volume, and they are right that a few easy hours a week is not a magic dose. Magness is talking about people trying to run a race well, often at higher volume, and he concedes that at very low volume some easy running should become steady. Where they really disagree is on evidence. Magness weights coaching history and performance. The review weights controlled mechanistic studies, which are short. Neither fully answers the other, and that is a limit of the literature, not of either argument. Brady Holmer's own short response was that a man is allowed to change his mind when presented with newer data.

A new meta-analysis bears on a related question: where to train relative to a threshold in order to move it. Ratko Peric and colleagues, in PeerJ on the twenty-second of October, pooled thirteen studies, twenty-four training groups and three hundred and thirty-eight participants. Training intensity relative to the threshold was the most consistent moderator. For the aerobic threshold, supra-threshold training improved it by thirty-eight point seven percent on average, at-threshold training by fourteen point seven, and sub-threshold by eighteen point four. For the anaerobic threshold, the graded pattern was two point six percent below, about seven at, and twelve point two above. On its face, this cuts against sub-threshold approaches. The caveats are large, and the authors state most of them. Heterogeneity was eighty-nine percent for the aerobic threshold, and the prediction interval ran from minus two to plus forty-nine percent. Lactate-based thresholds produced larger effects than ventilatory ones. The participants were untrained or amateur. And the authors write that chronic work near threshold "raises concerns about long-term effectiveness and non-functional overreaching in elite athletes", and that, untested, the best intensity may differ with fitness. The strongest case for this paper is that in short studies, harder sessions move thresholds more. The strongest case for sub-threshold training, as in the Norwegian model, is that it lets trained athletes accumulate far more threshold-adjacent work across years without breaking down. These studies do not test that.

Heat, durability and recovery

Two studies this week asked what happens late in a hard run, and one asked whether cold water helps you come back the next day.

Ben Hunter, Ed Maunder and colleagues, in the Scandinavian Journal of Medicine and Science in Sports, had ten trained runners, seven men and three women, average age thirty-eight, do incremental tests fresh and again after ninety minutes of running, at nineteen degrees and at thirty degrees, both at forty percent humidity. Fresh, the speed at the first ventilatory threshold was about seven percent lower in the heat, roughly zero point eight kilometres an hour. After ninety minutes it fell by eight point six percent in the cool and eleven point one in the warm, with no statistical interaction, so the heat did not accelerate the decline of the threshold itself. But running economy drifted more in the heat, by five point four percent from fifteen to ninety minutes against one point seven, and end-test speed fell by twelve point one percent against six point eight. The authors' summary is that after prolonged running in the warm, threshold speed sat on average seventeen point one percent below its fresh value in the cool. That number is the practical one. A runner who sets easy or threshold pace on a cool day and then runs long in the heat may be in a different intensity domain from the one intended. Ten runners, one humidity level, and intensity prescribed relative to each condition, so treat the detail cautiously.

A related heat paper came up on the Some Work, All Play podcast this week, which David and Megan Roche discussed. Peter McDonald, Julien Périard and colleagues, in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, had trained male cyclists do eight days of heat acclimation either in dry heat or in humid heat matched for wet-bulb globe temperature, then a thirty-minute time trial in both conditions. Both kinds of acclimation improved performance in both environments. In the dry time trial, dry acclimation added fourteen watts against six for humid, a further eight watts. In the humid time trial, the gains were similar, nine and twelve watts. Groups were small, ten and twelve men. For a runner preparing for a humid race without access to humidity, the practical reading is that ordinary heat training transfers, which was also the hosts' conclusion.

Brady Holmer, writing for Marathon Handbook, covered a small trial by Paula Brozinga and colleagues in the International Journal of Sports Medicine. Seventeen recreational male runners did an eleven-day block, two five-day blocks with a rest day between, each session thirty minutes of continuous running, one maximal three hundred metres and ten more three hundred metre intervals. Afterwards, one group sat in ten degree water for fifteen minutes, the other sat and rested. Soreness and blood markers of muscle damage rose. Three hundred metre times did not change in either group, and cold water made no difference to performance, soreness, creatine kinase or perceived effort. It is a small study, all men, and assignment details are not in the abstract. Holmer's practical suggestion is a good one: during a hard block, record both how sore the legs feel and a repeatable performance, because in this study they told different stories.

The same day, Holmer covered a cross-sectional study from Kexin Fang and colleagues in Frontiers in Physiology, of a hundred and forty-one men aged twenty-eight to forty living in a cold region, split at the group's median three thousand metre time of fourteen minutes thirteen seconds. After adjusting for limb muscle mass and correcting for multiple comparisons, ten of six hundred and thirty-six serum metabolites differed between faster and slower men, including higher cortisol in the faster group, and no metabolic pathway survived correction. There was no measure of diet or V O two max. Holmer's question for anyone selling a performance blood panel is the right one: when people change training or diet on the result, do their times improve? This study cannot say.

Fuelling

Last week we covered the training behind Andreas Almgren's New York debut. This week Maurten, his sponsor, published the carbohydrate data from his build, on a page that also sells the products, and Marathon Handbook reported it. On two long runs in August and September he drank thirty-six grams of carbohydrate every fifteen minutes from a drink containing a naturally occurring carbon thirteen tracer, and breath samples tracked how much of it he oxidised. On the first, a thirty-seven kilometre run at an average of three fifteen per kilometre, the drink supplied three percent of the carbohydrate he burned at five kilometres, ten percent at ten, about a quarter from sixteen kilometres, and twenty-nine percent at thirty. The peak oxidation rate from the drink was one point four grams a minute, eighty-three grams an hour. Maurten estimated his total carbohydrate use at two hundred and twenty-two grams an hour, so stored glycogen did most of the work. Across both runs, the drink never contributed more than eighty-seven grams an hour, against an intake Marathon Handbook puts at a hundred and forty-four on his hardest runs. In Almgren's words, "You can't store enough carbohydrates to be able to utilize everything for the entire workout, you need extra fueling." Maurten's model said that without fuel he would run short at marathon pace after about eighty minutes. In New York he planned about a hundred and twenty grams an hour.

On Some Work, All Play, David and Megan Roche discussed the same data and the wider move to high carbohydrate intake among elite endurance athletes. This is one athlete, tested by a company with a commercial interest, and the results of a third test at altitude were still being analysed. But it fits the controlled evidence. Tim Podlogar and colleagues, in the European Journal of Applied Physiology in twenty twenty-two, gave eleven trained male cyclists either a hundred and twenty or ninety grams an hour during three hours at moderate intensity. Exogenous oxidation in the last hour was higher with the bigger dose, one point five one against one point two nine grams a minute, but oxidation of the body's own stores did not differ. More carbohydrate in meant more carbohydrate burned from the bottle, not more glycogen spared. Two practical points follow, and both are labelled as my inference. First, the lag: with the drink contributing little for the first forty minutes or so, fuelling from the start of a long effort makes sense, which is also Maurten's own advice. Second, intake above what can be oxidised has to go somewhere, which is why gut tolerance is the usual limit, and Almgren said he had no stomach problems.

Brady Holmer's Physiology Friday covered the other end of carbohydrate availability: a randomised crossover trial by Kristine Lange, Jørn Helge and colleagues in the Journal of Physiology. Ten trained men did a long glycogen-depleting session, then ate either high or low carbohydrate for the rest of the day, and were retested the next morning with muscle biopsies. Peak fat oxidation rose after both, by twenty-four percent after high carbohydrate and ninety percent after low. The intensity at which fat oxidation peaked moved only after the low-carbohydrate day, from forty-one to fifty-six percent of V O two peak. Maximal power was about seven percent lower, and effort felt harder. And the mitochondria did not change: coupling control and efficiency were the same in both arms. Holmer's line captures it. A higher fat oxidation reading after a low-glycogen day is "a snapshot of fuel partitioning", not "a change in cellular machinery". The practical point for anyone who sets training zones from a lab fat-max test is that the result depends on what was eaten the day before. Ten men, two days, so this is about acute fuel selection, not long-term adaptation.

A systematic review and meta-analysis from Loughborough, by Freya Gibbons, Stephen Mears and colleagues, published in Sports Medicine on Tuesday the tenth, asked whether adding protein to a drink helps hydration. Sixteen studies, two hundred and five participants. In rehydration studies, protein-containing drinks increased fluid retention with a standardised mean difference of zero point six six, reduced urine output by zero point seven one, improved net fluid balance by zero point five eight, and increased plasma volume change by a smaller zero point two nine. Mean protein content was about twenty-four grams per litre. The authors flag two limits: only seventeen percent of participants were women, and there were no plant-based drinks, which they say limits generalisation to female athletes and to people whose diets exclude dairy. The mechanism is plausible, through slower gastric emptying and oncotic pressure, but these are short laboratory studies of fluid balance, not performance.

Last in fuelling, a meta-analysis in P LOS One by Jinfa Gu and colleagues pooled nine studies of vitamin D supplementation in three hundred and twelve football and futsal players. Supplementation raised blood levels substantially, with a Hedges' g of one point four one. It did nothing measurable for jump, sprint, aerobic capacity or strength, with all confidence intervals crossing zero, and the certainty was low to very low. The abstract reports no analysis by baseline status, season or latitude. Correcting a deficiency is a health question in its own right, and this paper says it is not a performance aid in already-trained players.

Strength: a book against its citations

Barbell Medicine's podcast this week, published on Wednesday the eleventh, is a review of Andrew Huberman's new book, Protocols. Jordan Feigenbaum read the book and pulled the studies it cites for the protocols that overlap with their work, and Austin Baraki guessed what the book recommends before hearing what the citations found. The show notes stress that they are rating what the book tells people to do, not Huberman personally. I have not read the book, so everything about its contents here is as Feigenbaum reads it on the podcast.

The lifting protocol, as Feigenbaum quotes it, is to train each major muscle group directly only once a week, with four to eight hard sets, and the book says of it, according to the show notes, "I've seen it work for everyone I've suggested it to." Feigenbaum's point is that the book's own citations point the other way. One is a twenty twenty-two systematic review by Eneko Baz-Valle and colleagues in the Journal of Human Kinetics, which I have checked. It pooled six randomised trials in trained young men and concluded that "a range of twelve to twenty weekly sets per muscle group may be an optimum standard recommendation" for hypertrophy, with no difference between moderate and high volume for the quadriceps or biceps and an advantage for higher volume in the triceps. That is a small review, but it does not support four to eight sets as optimal.

The one study cited for once-a-week training is a twenty twenty-four trial by Helene Pedersen, Brad Schoenfeld, Vidar Andersen and colleagues in the European Journal of Sport Science. Twenty-two resistance-trained adults did the same weekly lower-body work, four multi-joint exercises at four to five sets each, either in one session or spread over four. After eight weeks, squat one-rep max rose by fifteen kilograms with four sessions against eight with one, a significant difference of seven kilograms. Muscle thickness and jump height improved in both groups with no difference between them. So the study supports once a week as viable for hypertrophy and argues against it for strength. The book also concedes, Feigenbaum says, that stopping a rep or two short of failure still works, citing this year's American College of Sports Medicine position stand.

The cold plunge section produced a disagreement worth showing. The book cites a study of winter swimmers for a claim about brown fat. That study, by Susanna Søberg and colleagues in Cell Reports Medicine in twenty twenty-one, compared about eight young men who winter swim with eight who do not. Feigenbaum points out that it measured brown fat glucose uptake on a P E T scan, which shows activity rather than amount, at one time point, with no follow-up, and the abstract reports that the swimmers showed no brown fat activity at a comfortable temperature. On the wider evidence, he quotes a twenty twenty-five meta-analysis as concluding that the purported benefits remain unsubstantiated. I opened that paper's abstract, by Tara Cain and colleagues in P LOS One, and it reads more mixed than that. Eleven studies, three thousand one hundred and seventy-seven participants. Inflammation rose immediately after immersion. Stress fell twelve hours afterwards but not at the other time points. A narrative synthesis found improved sleep quality and quality of life, not mood, and a twenty-nine percent reduction in sickness absence among people taking cold showers. The authors call the evidence base constrained by few trials, small samples and a lack of diversity. I could not find the phrase he quotes in the abstract; it may be in the full text. Both readings agree that the benefits are modest and uncertain. Feigenbaum's own bottom line on cold plunges was: if you like it, go for it.

Hybrid and strength research

Three papers this week for anyone combining lifting with running, or coaching people who do.

A systematic review in Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism by Nathalia Dias, Cleiton Libardi and colleagues counted who takes part in hypertrophy research. Of five hundred and seventy resistance training studies, seventy-two point two percent recruited only men, eleven percent only women, and sixteen point eight percent both. Among studies that included women, eight point five percent reported or accounted for menstrual cycle phase. Molecular analyses were nearly four times as common in men-only studies. There was modest improvement after twenty twenty. The authors note that relative hypertrophy responses look broadly similar between sexes, but the evidence base that coaching advice rests on is largely male.

A biomechanics study in the Journal of Applied Physiology by Kolja Weidlich, Adamantios Arampatzis and colleagues in Berlin measured Achilles tendon strain in eighteen adolescent athletes during four common plyometric jumps: countermovement, drop, hurdle and horizontal single-leg jumps. Although ankle moments differed between jumps, peak tendon strain did not, averaging nine to ten point seven percent, above the strain range the authors cite as effective for tendon adaptation. Their conclusion is that frequent maximal-effort plyometrics may contribute to Achilles overload in adolescents. My inference for adult runners who add jumps to their strength work is narrower: if all maximal jumps load the tendon similarly, choosing a gentler-looking jump does not reduce tendon strain much; volume and effort are the parameters to adjust. This was eighteen adolescents, and strain was estimated from skin markers and ultrasound.

The tirzepatide trial from the news segment belongs here too, as the clearest evidence this year that resistance training changes what a large weight loss does to strength.

Gear and technology

A rule question with wider reach. At Connecticut's high school cross-country State Open on the fifth of November, Quentin Koch of Xavier finished second and was disqualified for wearing a heart rate monitor on his arm. Marathon Handbook quotes the state association's rule, set in capitals: "ANY RUNNER WHO USES AN ILLEGAL AID OTHER THAN A WATCH, SUCH AS A HEART RATE MONITOR, SHALL BE DISQUALIFIED." The national federation's rules have allowed wearables since twenty nineteen and restrict only data sent to a runner during a race. As Marathon Handbook notes, a watch with an optical sensor collects much the same data as an arm band, and the rule does not say whether that watch is still just a watch. His coach said the band's data could be read only after the race, and that close to half the field wore something similar. Koch can wear the same band at the New England Championships on the fourteenth. The point beyond Connecticut is that rules written in terms of device types are aging badly when every watch is a sensor.
