Doping and governance

On Friday the Athletics Integrity Unit provisionally suspended Peres Jepchirchir, the reigning world champion and the Olympic champion from Tokyo. In the unit's words, posted on X, an out-of-competition sample "collected in Kenya prior to her recent Sydney Marathon victory, returned an adverse analytical finding for E P O". This is a provisional suspension. No violation has been established, and she is entitled to the process that follows. Jepchirchir won Sydney on the thirtieth of August by three minutes and forty seconds from Irine Cheptai, so if a violation is later found, that result is in question. The unit's own website was down for maintenance when I checked, so the wording comes from its post as reproduced by Marathon Handbook and from the BBC's report.

Two days earlier the unit banned Ruti Aga, the twenty nineteen Tokyo Marathon champion, for six years, and the breakdown is the instructive part. She tested positive for methylprednisolone, a glucocorticoid prohibited in competition, after winning a half marathon in China in March. That earned two years. The other four years are for tampering. According to the unit's release, she gave false information in an interview in April, then in May produced what the unit calls a forged or manipulated document to support a prescription. The release says the investigation, supported by the Ethiopian anti-doping authority, established that the document was forged. In other words, the cover story cost twice as much as the positive test.

Health and fitness news

This was a quiet week for large trials and guidelines, so this segment is short.

In JAMA, three authors from the Center for Science in the Public Interest published a viewpoint on the peptides that are widely sold online as research chemicals. In July, an advisory committee of the Food and Drug Administration discussed whether seven of them, including B P C one five seven and T B five hundred, should be added to the list of substances pharmacies may compound. The viewpoint argues about what that process means for drug approval. It is an opinion piece, not a new regulatory decision, and I read only its opening, not the full argument. The Fast Talk podcast spent part of this week's episode on the same products. On the podcast, the Fast Talk Labs panel hosted by Trevor Connor made the practical point that most of the injectable peptides promoted online lack F D A approval and rest mainly on animal or test-tube studies, and that buyers often can't know what is in the vial.

Retraction Watch reported on a tool called EditorCheck, presented at a digital libraries conference on the fifteenth of October. It matched handling editors with authors' past co-authorships and found that more than eight thousand papers in P LOS journals and around two and a half thousand in M D P I journals breached those publishers' own rules on editor-author collaboration. The lead author, Jules Di Scala, was careful to say that this does not show the reviews were dishonest, only that the policy was not followed. Both publishers name their handling editors, which is why they could be audited. Most journals don't.

And the journal Sports Medicine published an exchange on how to judge replication in sport science. Last year a project estimated that only about seven of twenty-five significant findings it re-tested in the field's leading journals replicated robustly. That paper carries a correction, which I checked: it fixes misspelled author names and one affiliation, and changes none of the findings. A commentary by Koerner and Staller now argues that the field has an observation problem as well as a methods problem: in their view, many sport contexts are not stable enough over time for a failed replication to be read as a false original. The original authors have replied, and the reply is paywalled, so I have not read it. This is a theoretical argument with no new data, but it bears on how much weight any single exercise study can carry.

Running science

Alex Hutchinson's column in Outside this week is about a year-long trial from the University of Liège, by Guillaume Abran and colleagues, published in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance. Habitual heel-strikers were randomised to four sessions of running retraining, to minimalist shoes, or to a control group that did some light stretching. Running economy was measured on a treadmill at two, six and twelve months.

At twelve months, economy had improved by ten and a half per cent in the retraining group, nine point three per cent in the minimalist group, and four point seven per cent in the controls. The authors are clear that there were no clear differences between the groups at any time point. Hutchinson's summary is that changing your form or your shoes works, "but so does, you know, running". He also reports that the trial recruited one hundred and forty runners, and the abstract reports results for sixty-one, so more than half were lost over the year. And these were low-mileage runners: Hutchinson gives their average as twelve to sixteen miles a week, which helps explain why everyone improved.

Two readings are possible here, and Hutchinson gives both. The cautious one: there is no evidence that the interventions beat simply running more. The generous one: the intervention groups improved about twice as much as the controls, and a trial of this size, after that attrition, may simply have lacked the power to show it. His practical point, which I think holds either way, is patience: most studies showing that a change of form costs economy lasted a few weeks, and this one suggests that judging a change needs about a year.

Second, a comparison many coaches have assumed but nobody had tested: is a marathon harder on the muscles than a trail race of similar effort? Mélanie Metra, Guillaume Millet and colleagues at Saint-Étienne, in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, had forty-one trained runners complete a simulated treadmill marathon, a simulated treadmill trail run, or a real trail run of about twenty-nine kilometres and fourteen hundred metres of climbing. Which condition they did depended on whether they specialised in road or trail. After the marathon, voluntary activation of the knee extensors fell by twenty-three and a half per cent, against nine and thirteen per cent after the two trail runs. Squat-jump height fell by eleven point seven per cent after the marathon and actually rose after the trail runs, and a blood marker of muscle damage was several times higher. Recovery was slower after the marathon on several measures. The calf muscles showed no difference. The obvious caveat is in the design: road specialists ran the marathon and trail specialists ran the trails, so the comparison is between disciplines and the people who choose them, not a clean crossover. Still, it argues against assuming that a hilly trail race, at equal effort, is the more damaging event.

Third, a paper from John Davis, who writes Running Writings, with Allison Gruber's group at Indiana. They put fifty-five healthy runners on an instrumented treadmill at twelve speeds, wearing consumer chest straps and foot pods, and used a musculoskeletal model to estimate the forces on the Achilles tendon and the kneecap joint. Of nine metrics the devices report, cadence, vertical oscillation and vertical ratio had the strongest associations with those internal forces, up to about half a body weight of force for each standard deviation of the metric. Ground contact time and leg stiffness were weaker. The authors' practical conclusion is that cadence is the most useful, because consumer devices measure it accurately. The limits are that the forces are modelled, not measured, the runners were healthy, and nothing here links any metric to injury.

Durability and duty factor

The Some Work, All Play podcast this week took as its main topic a study tying worsening fatigue resistance to rising duty factor, the share of each stride the foot spends on the ground.

The hosts, David and Megan Roche, were enthusiastic about it, and about using a running power meter to track form under fatigue. One of them summarised it this way on the podcast: runners whose duty factor deteriorated more had worse fatigue resistance. I have read the paper's abstract, and it doesn't report that. It reports that duty factor drifted on average across the group, not that the size of an individual's drift predicted how much they slowed. That may be in the full paper, but I haven't seen it, so treat the individual-level claim as unconfirmed. The practical suggestion on the podcast, to cue being light on your feet when tired and to use long uphill treadmill sessions to train it, is coaching opinion drawn from personal experience, and it is offered that way.

The study is by Antonio Cartón-Llorente, Felipe García-Pinillos and Diego Jaén-Carrillo in the European Journal of Sport Science. Twelve trained trail runners did three repeated blocks on a treadmill, each an hour of steady running at eighty-five per cent of lactate-threshold speed followed by a twelve-minute uphill time trial, with a Stryd foot pod recording throughout. In the steady running, the only thing that drifted was duty factor, from seventy-one point three to seventy-two point seven per cent between the first and third hour. In the uphill time trials, after adjusting for the slower speeds, contact time, duty factor, leg stiffness and vertical oscillation all shifted towards a softer, longer-contact stride. Running cost was preserved throughout. The authors call it preliminary evidence, which is fair for twelve runners, and it shows duty factor changing with fatigue, not that it causes the loss of performance.

A companion paper from the same Zaragoza group, in the Scandinavian Journal of Medicine and Science in Sports, compared fifty-two elite distance runners with fifty-two recreational runners matched for sex, age, height and mass. At treadmill speeds from ten to fifteen kilometres an hour, the elite runners had shorter ground contact, with effect sizes of about point five to point seven, more time in the air, and a more forefoot-oriented landing. Step rate and step length did not differ. It's cross-sectional, and the forces are estimated from the sensors, so it describes what elite runners look like rather than what anyone should copy.

Intensity, sex and the heart

There is no new concurrent-training study this week worth your time, so this segment covers three things that bear on how endurance work is prescribed and what it does.

First, intensity control. Detrended fluctuation analysis alpha one, usually shortened to D F A alpha one, is a heart rate variability index that has been proposed as a way to estimate the first and second ventilatory thresholds, at fixed values of about point seven five and point five. Claudio Nieto-Jiménez, James Cotter and colleagues, in Physiology International, tested this in seventeen triathletes in swimming, cycling and running, holding constant loads at thresholds measured by gas exchange and recording with a Polar H ten chest strap. At the group level the means were close to the proposed values: about point six five to point seven five at the first threshold, and point three seven to point four seven at the second. But the spread between individuals was wide, and the authors conclude that fixed values cannot identify a given athlete's thresholds. Their words: "group-level correspondence rather than individual-level validity". If you use this index, it is a population average, not your threshold.

Second, a study in Sports Medicine that will be quoted widely. Xinyu Liu, Meihan Guo and David Montero measured two hundred and ninety-four healthy adults, half of them women, matched for age and physical activity across ages eighteen to seventy-eight. They measured lean body mass, haemoglobin mass and blood volume, cardiac structure and function, blood pressure, and peak aerobic capacity on a cycle ergometer. Lean mass was linearly related to most of these, and the slopes were the same for women and men: women sat at the lower end of one shared line and men at the upper end. Across the whole sample, more than ninety per cent of the variables differed by sex. But in seventy people matched for lean mass, no cardiac variable and no measure of aerobic capacity differed between women and men.

The authors conclude that these differences are "statistically accounted for" by lean mass, irrespective of sex. Two cautions. This is cross-sectional, and accounting for something statistically is not the same as showing it causes the difference. And the matched comparison involved seventy people, and the abstract lists cardiac variables and aerobic capacity among those that converged, not haemoglobin mass or blood volume. My inference, which goes beyond the paper: the result describes oxygen delivery relative to the mass that uses it. It does not say that performance gaps in sport would close if lean mass were equal, because performance also depends on how much lean mass you have to carry and move.

Third, Brady Holmer's Physiology Friday this week is a long synthesis of what he calls the athlete's heart paradox: lifelong endurance athletes, mostly middle-aged men, have more coronary plaque than their peers, yet live longer. As he summarises the studies he has covered over the years, the Master at Heart study found that lifelong athletes had higher odds of several plaque types and more significant narrowing than non-athletes, though fewer vulnerable plaques. MARC two followed the same men for seven years and found that training volume did not predict plaque progression, but a larger share of very vigorous training was linked to faster calcium progression. And a randomised trial in patients with coronary disease found that supervised four-by-four-minute intervals reduced plaque volume slightly. Those figures are his summaries; I have not opened those papers this week. His conclusion: "Fitness lowers the risk that comes with plaque. It doesn't make plaque go away." And blood pressure, lipids and a high calcium score deserve the same attention in athletes as in anyone else. A reader points out in the comments that the studies are almost all of men, which is a fair criticism of the whole literature.

A new paper fits alongside it. Wioletta Sacharczuk and colleagues in Poznań compared forty-two recreational runners over forty, running at least twenty kilometres a week, with twenty-seven sedentary controls of similar age, in the European Journal of Applied Physiology. The ascending aorta was wider in the runners, three point two five against two point nine centimetres, and the difference held after adjusting for age, sex and height. No runner reached four centimetres, everything stayed within normal limits, and markers of collagen turnover did not differ. The authors read this as modest, exercise-related remodelling rather than disease. It's small and cross-sectional, and it says nothing about what happens over decades.

Barbell Medicine's podcast took up the same question from the other end: how much cardio is too much? Jordan Feigenbaum starts with the Copenhagen jogging study from twenty fifteen, the source of headlines saying fast running is as deadly as sitting on the couch. In the paper, the strenuous joggers had a hazard ratio of about two compared with sedentary non-joggers, and the show notes put that group at two deaths among forty people. Here he is on the podcast on what the confidence interval means.

[quoted clip]

The interval in the paper runs from point four eight to eight point one four, and the authors themselves said the strenuous group was not statistically different from the sedentary one. Against that he sets a pooled analysis of six cohorts by Hannah Arem and colleagues, with six hundred and sixty-one thousand people and a hundred and sixteen thousand deaths. Mortality was thirty-nine per cent lower at three to five times the guideline minimum than among the inactive, and there was no sign of harm at ten times or more. His argument is that self-reported minutes plateau, while studies that measure fitness directly have not found a ceiling. He identifies one harm he thinks holds up, atrial fibrillation. According to the episode notes, it appears with around six to seven hours a week of hard endurance training sustained for years. His own position, which he labels as such, is to do as much as time and recovery allow, with double the guidelines as a reasonable target.

Strength sports

Two papers this week on what drives muscle growth, and one that tests a claim behind menstrual-cycle training advice.

First, progressive overload. Witalo Kassiano, Edilson Cyrino and colleagues in Brazil, in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, had forty-one untrained young women train each arm separately for eight weeks: three sets of eight to twelve curls, three times a week. One arm added load whenever the woman reached twelve repetitions; the other kept the same load and repetitions throughout; some arms served as untrained controls. Both trained arms grew more than controls, and the progressively loaded arm grew more than the constant-load arm. The authors also measured muscle swelling immediately after a session as a rough proxy for how hard the muscle was worked. They interpret the result to mean that progression works by keeping each set challenging as the muscle adapts. My reading: that fits the general view that effort relative to capacity matters more than any particular load. The limits are a single muscle, untrained women, ultrasound thickness as the outcome, and a proxy for muscle use that is indirect.

Second, a direct test of an idea that turns up in a lot of menstrual-cycle programming: that high progesterone in the luteal phase is catabolic, so training and protein advice should change with the cycle. Alysha D'Souza, Stuart Phillips and colleagues at McMaster, in the Journal of Physiology, gave twenty young women either four hundred milligrams of oral progesterone or a placebo during the early follicular phase, which raised progesterone well above the normal luteal range. One leg did resistance exercise, both legs were biopsied, and everyone took thirty grams of whey. Exercise and protein both increased muscle protein synthesis, as expected. Progesterone made no detectable difference at rest, after feeding, or after exercise. The authors conclude that cycle-phase recommendations based on a progesterone effect on muscle are not supported by direct mechanistic evidence. The limits: one acute dose, twenty participants, and synthesis over hours rather than growth over months. But it removes the mechanism that much of that advice rests on.

Third, plant protein. Kevin Bischof and colleagues in Vienna, in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition, pooled fifty-one trials that measured muscle protein synthesis after resistance exercise and a single protein dose, and ranked protein sources against each other in a network meta-analysis. Native whey, pea protein, whole egg, mycoprotein and whey ranked highest, in that order. Only whey's effect reached statistical significance, many sources rest on a few studies, and certainty was rated low to moderate. The dose-response analysis put the best predicted responses at about eighteen to twenty-five grams for both the animal and the plant sources. For anyone on a plant-based diet, this is the acute evidence that pea and mycoprotein can match dairy protein. It's short-term synthesis, not muscle growth, and it says nothing about mixed plant meals.

Fuelling

The Real Science of Sport podcast spent its main segment on lactate drinks, and the occasion is a commercial launch. The Italian company Enervit has put on sale the drink mix the U A E team used at this year's Tour de France. According to the cycling site Brújula Bike, each dose contains ninety grams of carbohydrate as glucose and fructose plus ten grams of sodium lactate, about two thousand milligrams of sodium, and costs about eight and a half euros. The company says it was developed with the physiologist George Brooks, who originated the lactate shuttle theory, and with Iñigo San Millán. The pitch is that lactate is a third fuel absorbed separately from glucose and fructose, so it can add energy when the gut's carbohydrate capacity is full.

The hosts, Ross Tucker and his co-host, were sceptical, and they went through the evidence. I've checked the studies they cited. The most relevant, by Claire Bordoli and colleagues at Nottingham Trent in twenty twenty-four, gave sixteen trained male cyclists about a hundred and twenty milligrams of lactate per kilogram, close to the dose in this product for a seventy-kilogram rider, before repeated time trials. Performance did not change. Perceived exertion fell and blood bicarbonate rose, so the lactate salt worked as a mild buffer, and there were gastrointestinal side effects. A second, by Taylor Ewell and colleagues at Colorado State in twenty twenty-four, is the study the marketing uses: fifteen recreational exercisers did about four per cent more work in a twenty-minute time trial with a commercial lactate supplement. But the dose was nineteen milligrams per kilogram, under one and a half grams for a seventy-kilogram person. The hosts argue that this is far too little energy to explain a four per cent gain by fuelling, and I agree: whatever produced that result, it cannot have been the lactate as fuel. The study also found no change in peak oxygen uptake or thresholds, and the hosts point out that four of the fifteen did worse.

The strongest version of the case for lactate, which the hosts also made, is that it could matter if carbohydrate is genuinely queuing in the gut at very high intakes, or if lactate acts as a signal to the brain or as a buffer rather than as fuel. Those are open questions. On present evidence, though, the question is whether ten grams of lactate does anything that ten more grams of carbohydrate wouldn't, and nobody has tested that. The hosts also noted that Brooks has patents pending in this area. That does not make him wrong, but it means independent trials matter.

Two smaller fuelling papers. From Hacettepe University in Ankara, with Andy Jones as a co-author, in the International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism: twenty-one recreational women ran ten-kilometre time trials on an outdoor track after either sodium bicarbonate, at point three grams per kilogram, or a salt placebo, each taken with a carbohydrate meal ninety minutes beforehand and in the same phase of the cycle. They were about thirty-two seconds faster with bicarbonate, fifty minutes and forty-six seconds against fifty-one minutes and eighteen seconds, with no difference in gut symptoms. That's notable, in my reading, because bicarbonate is mostly studied in shorter, harder efforts, not fifty minutes of running. One caution: bicarbonate tastes distinctive, so blinding is hard.

And from Loughborough, in the Journal of Applied Physiology: eleven trained cyclists rode a hundred minutes in thirty-six degree heat, drinking sixty grams an hour of labelled glucose either ice cold or at body temperature, then did a time trial. Cold drinks lowered heart rate and core temperature, but they did not change how much of the ingested carbohydrate was oxidised, and time-trial performance did not differ. So cold fluids reduce strain in the heat without improving carbohydrate use, at least at sixty grams an hour.

Last in this segment: Jack Norris, a registered dietitian, has rewritten the sports nutrition review on VeganHealth dot org. It now covers endurance, recovery, iron, energy and fibre, bone stress injuries, creatine, taurine and collagen, and it sets an explicit evidence bar, listing studies it excluded and why. Norris also discloses that he used A I for much of the literature review and checking. A few points are worth passing on for anyone on a plant-based diet.

On protein, the review finds that vegans and omnivores eating similar amounts of protein had similar gains in muscle and strength, and that about one gram per kilogram per day appears enough to build or maintain muscle in the limited studies, mostly in beginners. It cites a new Wageningen trial in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition by Jacintha Domić and colleagues. Seventy-two healthy adults aged sixty-five and over were randomised to their usual omnivorous diet, a self-selected vegan diet, or a self-selected vegan diet plus resistance training, for twelve weeks. On the vegan diet without training, protein intake fell by about nineteen to twenty-eight grams a day, and thigh muscle volume fell by point six of a litre, against no change on the omnivorous diet. With resistance training the loss was point three five of a litre, a little over half as much. The lesson is not that plant protein fails but that switching without planning protein, especially in older adults, can leave intake short.

On creatine, the review points out that vegans get none from food and have lower muscle stores. A new trial by Miguel López-Moreno and colleagues in the European Journal of Applied Physiology gave twenty active vegans creatine or a placebo for four weeks. Jump performance improved; sprinting and strength tests did not. The abstract's conclusion says creatine "enhances muscle strength", which its own results section does not support, and the review reads it correctly. Serum creatinine also rose in the creatine group. That is expected, because creatinine is a breakdown product of creatine, and my inference is that it matters for anyone having a kidney function test, since the standard estimate of kidney function is calculated from creatinine and so can read falsely low while supplementing.

On collagen, the review finds the trials on joint pain and recovery mostly funded by manufacturers and without a meaningful benefit, and notes that collagen supplements are not vegan in any case. Adequate protein and vitamin C support the body's own collagen synthesis.

Recovery claims that didn't hold up

Alex Hutchinson's other column this week is three studies that didn't find what people hoped. He makes the point that writing only about positive results is how publication bias reaches readers.

The first is from K U Leuven, the group that has done most of the work on ketones for recovery, in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise. Eighteen well-trained cyclists rode an eight-day simulated stage race, taking twenty-five grams of ketone ester or a taste-matched placebo after each stage and before bed. The race produced real fatigue, with time-trial performance down about three per cent, but ketones made no difference to recovery, sleep, cognitive function or performance. The groups were small and unequal, eight on ketones and ten on placebo. Hutchinson's reading is the right one: even if ketones help, the effect "can't be enormous or it would have shown up here".

The second, which Hutchinson attributes to Luc van Loon's group at Maastricht, tested whether red and near-infrared light, now marketed as photobiomodulation, improves mitochondrial function in human muscle. As he describes it, twelve people had one leg treated and the other given a sham; muscle and skin biopsies showed no difference beyond slight surface warming. I could not find the paper itself, so that is his account.

The third, again as Hutchinson describes it, from a French group led by Thomas Rupp and Sébastien Racinais in the Journal of Sports Sciences: twenty-two people did a simulated football match, then had twenty-five minutes in either an ice bath or a hot tub, immediately and on the following two days. Soreness, fatigue and neuromuscular function did not differ. The authors frame hot water as an effective alternative to cold. Hutchinson's reading is that if doing a thing and doing its opposite give the same result, probably neither does much. I couldn't locate this paper either.

A narrative review in Sports Medicine this week adds a timing question. Panagiotis Miliotis, Shona Halson and colleagues suggest that cold exposure within about two hours of bed may delay sleep, by reducing the temperature gradient between the hands and feet and the body's core that helps sleep onset, while cold taken earlier, or heat, might help. It's a narrative review built on heterogeneous studies, and one author works for Whoop, so read it as a set of hypotheses rather than advice.
