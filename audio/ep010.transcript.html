Almgren's debut, and a form nobody would sign

This week the New York City Marathon supplies most of the news and a useful pair of training logs. Andreas Almgren won on his debut after a block built on double threshold days and very long hard runs. Jess McClain finished second on one workout and one structured long run a week. So the first part of this episode asks what two very different preparations can and cannot tell anyone.

After that: the death of a runner in New York and what the research on marathon rhabdomyolysis does and does not say; new ferritin thresholds for iron deficiency; a policy change on injectable peptides; three doping and governance stories; a long look at a young footballer told to give up his sport, and what is known about heart screening; then concurrent training, kidney markers on very high protein diets, Smith machine squats, hot baths, Achilles rehabilitation and one wide-fit shoe.

A note on method. Most of the papers below were read at abstract level, and podcasts were read through machine transcripts, so I will say so where it matters. The reading pile had nothing in scope this week, so there is no segment from it. It runs a little under the usual hour, because I have not stretched it to fit.

Racing and competition

New York, on Sunday the first of November. Andreas Almgren of Sweden won in two hours, six minutes and one second, in his first marathon. Yemaneberhan Crippa of Italy was second in two, six, fourteen. Kamohelo Mofolo of Lesotho took third on his twenty first birthday in two, six, twenty six, catching the defending champion Benson Kipruto by just over half a second. Almgren finished four seconds outside the Swedish record, and Marathon Handbook says he is the first man to win New York on debut since Gebre Gebremariam in twenty ten.

The race was decided very late. Ten men went through thirty five kilometres within a second of each other. Almgren and Crippa were given the same time at twenty five miles. All thirteen seconds of the winning margin came after that point: a twenty sixth mile in four minutes twenty five, against Crippa's four thirty two, and the last three hundred and fifty two metres in fifty six seconds. His second half took one hour, one minute and fifty seven seconds after a first half of one, four, oh four. Marathon Handbook went back through the official splits for every winner since twenty fourteen and found no faster second half.

The women's race was just as close at the end. Sheila Chepkirui of Kenya won her second title in three years in two, twenty four, thirty six. Jess McClain of the United States was six seconds back in two, twenty four, forty two, and beat the defending champion Hellen Obiri by four tenths of a second, catching her on the short rise in the last two hundred metres. McClain is the first American woman in the top three since Shalane Flanagan in twenty eighteen. The first five kilometres took eighteen minutes twenty three, and at thirty five kilometres six women were still together, including Edna Kiplagat, who is forty six. Then Chepkirui ran the next five kilometres in sixteen twenty four, the fastest of the race. Weini Kelati, who had been second at thirty kilometres, lost more than a minute over that stretch and finished seventh in two, twenty six, fifty one on her debut.

The race also set a participation record. Of the one thousand nine hundred and twenty additional finishers compared with last year, one thousand seven hundred and eighty six were women. London responds next April with a one-off two-day race and one hundred thousand places, with men and women running on separate days.

Two ways to run a marathon

The most useful thing to come out of New York is the record of how Almgren prepared, because he writes his own programme, tests his own lactate and talks about it in detail. Marathon Handbook pulled together his training log, interviews and watch data, the last supplied by COROS, the maker of his watch. That is one athlete, a company's data, and a journalist's reconstruction. It is a description, not evidence that the method caused the result.

Over the ten weeks after his European ten thousand metre title he ran two thousand and six kilometres, with a highest week of two hundred and forty one. He kept two double threshold days a week but changed both sessions. The morning short repetitions became fifty minutes of continuous running at his first lactate threshold. The afternoon session became longer, for example sixteen times one kilometre. On the first of September he ran the first eight of those at two fifty per kilometre with a reading of one point five millimoles, and the last eight at two forty seven with a reading of about two and a half. In one documented week he also ran three fifty minute sessions in a heat chamber at thirty eight degrees and sixty percent humidity, holding his core temperature at about thirty eight and a half.

The change the article considers most significant was the long run. Before this block he had never run farther than about thirty two kilometres. He then did a series of hard long runs, including a forty kilometre run divided into four tens of thirty five minutes, thirty one twenty, twenty nine forty six and twenty eight thirty, and he held back slightly during them so that he could recover within two days and do more of them.

The race data matches the training claim. The paces he ran in New York correspond, in Marathon Handbook's reading, to paces where his training lactate was between about one and one and a half millimoles. His average heart rate was one hundred and fifty six in the first half and one hundred and sixty three in the second, against a threshold heart rate COROS had measured at one hundred and sixty eight in August, while his pace went from three oh two to two fifty six per kilometre. Cadence rose from one hundred and seventy eight to one hundred and eighty three, and ground contact time fell from one hundred and forty seven milliseconds to one hundred and forty one. Over the final six hundred metres the watch shows two thirty seven per kilometre and one hundred and twenty milliseconds of contact, though a watch measuring six hundred metres of its own distance has some margin of error. The official splits support the broad point: from forty kilometres to the line he averaged about two forty four and a half, a little quicker than his fifty eight minute half marathon pace. My reading, not the article's: heart rate below threshold for most of a marathon at this speed says something about how far he was from his limit, but single-race heart rate is affected by heat, drift and excitement, so I would not treat seven beats as a measurement of anything very precise.

His fuelling plan is worth reporting with care, since these are plans he described and not confirmed intake: a bicarbonate drink two hours before the start, a caffeine gel before the start and another at halfway, and a bottle at each five kilometre station through thirty five, each delivering about thirty grams. He had practised at about one hundred and twenty grams of carbohydrate an hour, even on easy long runs. Episode nine covered a bicarbonate trial over ten kilometres. Here is another, from Feuerbacher and colleagues with Bent Rønnestad, in the European Journal of Sport Science: a randomised, double blind crossover in twenty four well trained cyclists taking about zero point two six grams per kilogram. Power output in high intensity intervals was two percent higher, and time above ninety percent of V O two max rose by about twenty four seconds. Cycling economy and fractional utilisation did not change. The authors say plainly that whether this turns into better long term adaptation has not been shown, and nobody has tested it in a marathon debut in New York.

Now the other preparation. Jess McClain is coached by David Roche, and on the Some Work, All Play podcast the Roches described her week: one quality workout and one structured long run that serves as a second workout, with truly easy days, and by their account no huge mileage. In the machine transcript the hosts say her easy days have no hidden work in them, and that when this was described to reporters after the race the reaction was surprise. Marathon Handbook adds that she still works a job outside running, and gives her record: second in New York, after two twenty seven nineteen in the same race two years ago, and two twenty forty nine at Boston in April, on a course that is not record eligible.

The strongest case for Almgren's method is that threshold work controlled by lactate allows a very large volume of quality with little damage, and that a thirty one year old whose injuries kept him out of three Olympic Games is now running better than ever. The strongest case for McClain's is that she has kept improving at every major on a much simpler week, after a sacral stress fracture in twenty twenty two changed how she approached training. I cannot compare them. They differ in sex, age, event history, life constraints, and in almost every other variable. The Roches themselves, on the podcast, say Almgren's work at his first threshold resembles what they try to do with uphill treadmill sessions: a lot of output with less breakdown. That is a coach's analogy, not a result. Steve Magness's podcast this week, The Good-Enough Approach to Double Threshold, argues, going by the show's own description, that the value of the Norwegian method lies in the principle rather than the copied sessions, and it names survivorship bias. I have read the description, not a transcript.

The sharpest disagreement this week comes from triathlon. Joel Filliol, who has coached four athletes to seven world titles, was on That Triathlon Show. His squad uses a flat training structure, does few lab tests and no lactate testing, and spreads its hard sessions across the week. His central claim is about dose.

Asked about the threshold volume described by Marius Bakken on the same show two weeks earlier, Filliol said on the podcast that the amount of it, in his words, feels like a lot to me, in the context he works in, because triathletes are recovering from far more total energy use.

Filliol is talking about triathletes, with far more total training hours, so this is not a straight contradiction of Bakken, whose method is built around running. He said so himself, and he said he finds the book interesting. But the two do differ on how much intensity is needed and how it should be controlled: Bakken by lactate and muscle feel, Filliol by pace ranges from Jack Daniels' tables, with a fixed ceiling on the final block. He also said he trusts readiness scores less than most athletes do, calling them black box algorithms that can cause more stress than they solve. All of this is a coach's experience. Filliol has the titles, and Almgren is this week's example for the other side. Marco Altini made a compatible point about individual differences, in his report from a fifty two kilometre mountain race: he improved by almost an hour with no detectable change in the usual determinants of road performance, which he attributes to specific volume and mechanical durability. That is one person's account, from a careful observer, and he says plainly it does not necessarily apply to others.

One more paper fits here, because it is about concentrated intensity. Sørensen and colleagues, again from the Rønnestad group, assigned seventeen well trained female cross country skiers and biathletes either to five high intensity sessions over six days followed by five days of active recovery, or to their regular training. The block group improved more on two of three roller ski performance measures, by three point six percent against one point two and by four point four percent against zero, and the third did not differ significantly. On the Fast Talk podcast this week the panel criticised what appears to be a different Rønnestad block study in cyclists, which I could not locate, on the grounds that one group came into the post test rested and the other did not. I raise it because the same question applies here. The abstract says the block replaced part of habitual training and reduced overall training load, and the recovery period is part of the protocol. My inference: some of that gain may be freshness on test day. A longer wash-out would be needed to separate the two.

General health and fitness news

The saddest story of the week first. Rachel Apa, twenty four, collapsed near mile twenty four of the New York City Marathon, her first. Spectators began C P R and race medical staff used a defibrillator. She died that evening at Mount Sinai after about six hours of treatment. Her father told the New York Times the cause was rhabdomyolysis. As Marathon Handbook notes, everything known so far about the cause comes from her family. There is no published medical finding, and I will not speculate about it.

There is one directly relevant paper, published in April in Nephron by physicians from Mount Sinai and Lenox Hill. It reviewed records at four New York hospitals from twenty seventeen to twenty twenty three for marathon runners with a creatine kinase above five thousand. In twenty twenty two, the hottest New York marathon on record, there were twenty four cases, against fifteen in the other five races combined. Cases correlated strongly with race day heat index, and the authors suggest the jump in temperature compared with the previous fortnight may add risk. The twenty twenty two cases were mostly men, twenty of twenty four, averaged thirty two years old and came in within two days. One needed dialysis. This is a retrospective chart review that can only find people who came to those hospitals. This year's race was much cooler, so the heat finding does not obviously apply, and one case cannot be explained by a population study either way.

Second, iron. The American Society of Hematology has issued its first evidence-based guideline on diagnosing iron deficiency. It is in the November issue of Blood Advances, after appearing online earlier. It recommends a ferritin threshold of thirty nanograms per millilitre or lower for most adults, double the fifteen commonly used, with up to fifty appropriate for adults with symptoms or ongoing risk factors, whether or not they are anaemic. That is a large change, and the caveats are large too. According to a clinical summary I read, all five recommendations were conditional, resting on low or very low certainty evidence, except a strong recommendation against using fifteen in pregnancy, which was also low certainty. The authors admit limited direct evidence tying specific ferritin values to outcomes that matter to patients. It is not written for athletes. My inference for endurance athletes: more results will now read as deficient, which is useful only if someone also asks why and whether treatment changes anything. Ferritin is an acute phase reactant, so a reading taken soon after a hard race may overstate stores. On the podcast covered later in this episode, Austin Baraki said the revision matches what he had already been arguing for in practice. That is a physician's opinion, not trial evidence.

Third, a development on a story from episode nine. Then it was a JAMA viewpoint on the F D A's advisory committee and injectable peptides. On the twenty eighth of October the US Department of Health and Human Services issued an enforcement discretion policy for compounding six of them: B P C one five seven, epitalon, K P V, M O T S C, semax and T B five hundred. According to Fierce Pharma, compounders must meet sourcing conditions and report serious adverse events, and the department may revisit the policy if safety signals appear. These are not approved drugs. They have not been formally added to the bulk substances list, so this is a decision not to enforce, rather than a finding that they work or are safe. Supporters call it a more accountable route than the grey market, and critics say it undercuts the F D A's own resistance. My inference: an American health agency's enforcement stance has no bearing on anti-doping rules, and I did not check the status of each compound on the prohibited list this run, so nobody should assume from this news that any of them is permitted in tested sport.

Fourth, retatrutide. Eli Lilly released topline results from a head to head phase three trial against injectable semaglutide, in adults with type two diabetes, starting at an average of about one hundred and two kilograms. At eighty weeks A one C fell by two point two points against one point eight, and body weight by nineteen and a half percent on nine milligrams and twenty point nine percent on twelve, against nine point four percent. Discontinuation for adverse events was four point eight and seven point nine percent, against two point seven. This is a company press release, these are efficacy estimand figures, and the details have not been peer reviewed. The part of the release I read says nothing about how much of that weight was lean mass, which is what would matter most to anyone who trains. Fast Talk's panel this week took on whether endurance athletes should use these drugs at all, and said it could find no specific research in endurance athletes. They were clear that the drugs help people at metabolic risk, and that most people regain the weight on stopping. I agree that the evidence for using them to get lighter for racing does not exist.

Fifth, something about how anyone finds evidence, including this programme. Retraction Watch reports that the National Library of Medicine has paused indexing Scientific Reports in MEDLINE, and archiving in PubMed Central, while it re-evaluates the journal. Its editor's email cites significant growth in recent years. The most recent articles in PubMed are from the fifteenth of September. The journal has twenty eight thousand, five hundred and ninety three articles indexed in PubMed this year, and has published forty one thousand, one hundred and twenty two. The library's policy says re-evaluation typically takes seven to ten months, and it did not give a reason. My inference: for this digest, whose literature searches run largely on PubMed, any Scientific Reports paper since mid September will simply not appear.

Doping and governance

A follow-up to episode nine, when Peres Jepchirchir's provisional suspension and Ruti Aga's six year ban were the news. The Athletics Integrity Unit has now banned Mosisa Siyoum of Ethiopia for eight years. Four are for E P O in a sample taken after he won a mile in Saudi Arabia last December, and four are for tampering. He had explained the positive with medical records saying a health centre gave him erythropoietin for anaemia. According to Marathon Handbook's account of the decision, the centre replied that the record number was not in its format, the signatory was not on its staff, and he had not been there on that date. His three thirty four point two eight personal best is gone. He did not contest it, though an appeal to the Court of Arbitration for Sport remains possible. Fake paperwork has now added four years twice in a fortnight.

Jepchirchir's case is still provisional, and the sample came from an out-of-competition test in Kenya about six weeks before her Sydney win. Athletics Kenya has restated a zero tolerance policy. Two countries have moved on the suppliers. Kenya's Anti-Doping Amendment Bill, now at public hearings, would raise minimum penalties for supplying or administering banned substances to five million shillings, about thirty eight thousand dollars, or five years in prison, or both, up from three million and three years. It covers doctors, pharmacists and physiotherapists, and a separate clause sets minimum penalties for people using banned substances in gyms. India's sports minister says a bill creating criminal penalties for suppliers goes to the next session of Parliament, with up to five years in prison, and athletes themselves would not be prosecuted. By April, India had one hundred and forty eight athletes on the Athletics Integrity Unit's list, two more than Kenya.

The Real Science of Sport spent half an hour on what this all means, and the disagreement there is useful. The strongest version of the case for hard sanctions is that Kenya produces a steady run of positives at the top of the sport and has just been awarded the twenty twenty nine world championships. Ross Tucker, on the podcast, rejects stripping them. His argument is about incentives: if hosting depends on fewer positive tests, the cheapest way to get them is to catch fewer people, so punishment falls on the body that has let testers work rather than on those supplying drugs. The hosts also recall Brett Clothier of the Athletics Integrity Unit describing Kenyan doping as diffuse rather than centrally organised, a market problem. That is not the same emphasis as WADA president Witold Bańka's, as quoted on the show, that this should not be seen as only a Kenyan or African problem, although both end up pointing at suppliers. Tucker separately argues against banning agents merely by association, while saying they should be pursued if there is evidence. And he reads, from a message sent to him, that the twenty twenty seven World Anti-Doping Code will require automatic investigation when support personnel have worked with more than one athlete who commits a violation. I have not checked that against the Code itself. These are opinions from a machine transcript without speaker labels, so treat the attribution as approximate.

World Athletics, meanwhile, has created a department for road running and mass participation, led by Jakob Larsen. More than three hundred and twenty road races hold a label. Under the twenty twenty seven rules a Platinum marathon pays seventy five thousand dollars, up from fifty thousand, with a two percent levy on top prize money. World Athletics says this brings in three million dollars a year for road racing's anti-doping programme, and a Platinum race must also fund at least twelve in-competition tests. My inference: whether three million dollars a year is sized to the problem is a fair question.

The form nobody would sign

The best hour of audio this week is the Barbell Medicine podcast's mystery case, with Jordan Feigenbaum presenting a published case to Austin Baraki blind. It is a case report from Portugal, so it is one person. A young soccer player with no symptoms needs a form signed. His E C G shows voltage criteria for left ventricular hypertrophy, with inverted T waves across many leads and S T depression. Imaging shows thickening at the apex of the left ventricle. Everything functional looks fine: no arrhythmia on the monitor, an exceptional treadmill test, no outflow obstruction, and a negative panel of ten genes. He is told to stop. After four months without sport, the E C G has improved and the wall has thinned enough that it no longer meets the diagnostic threshold.

Most people would now call that athlete's heart, since the textbook test is to remove the training and see what regresses. The title of the case report says otherwise: beware of regression, it may not always mean athlete's heart. The report's authors say it challenges the dogma that such regression is exclusive to physiological remodelling. His team kept the diagnosis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and he stopped playing. The podcast then gives a second case, of a young hockey player whose thickening regressed substantially, but who had a gene mutation and scarring on imaging, and later needed a defibrillator. Feigenbaum's conclusion is that detraining can help rule the disease in but cannot rule it out. That is a clinician's inference from a handful of cases.

Reference ranges are the next problem. The classic upper limit comes from Pelliccia and colleagues in nineteen ninety one, which episode six discussed, in Italian athletes. Feigenbaum points out this is not the whole athletic population. Basavarajaiah and colleagues, in a British study, compared three hundred nationally ranked Black male athletes with three hundred matched white male athletes. Mean wall thickness was eleven point three against ten millimetres. Eighteen percent of the Black athletes had walls over twelve millimetres against four percent of the white athletes, and three percent had walls of fifteen or more against none. The authors warn that applying limits from white athletes could generate false positive diagnoses, and they also note that deaths from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are more prevalent among Black athletes, so the error can run in both directions. All male, and that is a real limit of the study.

Riding and colleagues then showed the word Black covers too much. In one thousand six hundred and ninety eight male athletes, left ventricular hypertrophy appeared in nine and a half percent of African American and Caribbean athletes and five percent of West Africans, but none of the East or North Africans, a result that held after accounting for body size. Abnormal repolarisation ranged from about one to eight and a half percent by region. Their conclusion is that the collective term should not imply that all Black athletes' hearts are comparable. My inference: anyone reading an athlete's E C G or echo needs ancestry more specific than a census box, and someone who has been given an alarming result is entitled to ask which reference population was used.

Then the question of whether a diagnosis should end a career. Per the show, for forty years guidelines largely said yes. Lampert and colleagues followed one thousand six hundred and sixty people with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or the genotype, at forty two centres. Forty two percent exercised vigorously. The composite of death, cardiac arrest, arrhythmic syncope and appropriate shocks occurred in four point seven percent of vigorous exercisers and four point six percent of the rest, an adjusted hazard ratio of one point oh one, with an upper confidence limit of one point four eight. Exercise was self-reported and not randomised, people chose their own activity, and they were treated at experienced centres, as the authors say. So this cannot show that vigorous exercise is safe for someone assessed elsewhere, or for every phenotype. Per the show, the twenty twenty five American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology statement no longer treats the diagnosis as an absolute bar. Baraki and Feigenbaum both think this patient's low risk features might support shared decision making today, though Baraki says it is outside his routine practice.

Should an E C G be mandatory? The two pieces of evidence pull apart. Harmon and colleagues pooled fifteen studies and forty seven thousand athletes: E C G sensitivity ninety four percent against twenty percent for history and nine percent for examination, with a lower false positive rate. So it is by far the better detector. Halkin and colleagues modelled copying the Italian programme in the United States: between ten point six and fourteen point four million dollars per life saved. The modelling rests on one Italian study, and it is an American cost calculation. Being good at detecting disease is not the same as saving lives economically. Feigenbaum is not opposed to the test, only to making amateurs pay for it, and he then says what he would do instead.

Hybrid and concurrent training

Gomes and colleagues in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research randomised thirty trained young men to endurance, resistance or concurrent training for eleven weeks, with a shift in the second part from maximal to explosive strength. Maximal isometric leg press force rose only in the resistance group, by eighteen percent. Peak rate of force development improved in both resistance and concurrent groups, but early phase force development in the concurrent group improved only in the second block. Cross-sectional area of the vastus lateralis rose seventeen percent with resistance training and eight point eight with concurrent, both significant, with wide variation. Jump impulse improved in both. So there is interference in maximal force and perhaps size, and the authors see none in jump performance. Ten or so men per arm can only give a rough idea, and the abstract does not give a direct comparison between arms for every outcome.

Trane and colleagues, from Norway, randomised fifty eight recreationally active people not trained in strength to two weekly sprint sessions, alone or with heavy squats, hypertrophy squats, or squat jumps, for eight weeks. Running economy improved two and a half percent with heavy squats, more than with sprinting alone, and leg press force by fourteen and a half percent. Short sprint time improved by about a tenth of a second with either kind of squat. Squat jumps did not match them. Note the authors' own words: an exploratory design with no strong multiplicity control, and hypothesis generating. These were not runners, and sprint work is not distance training. It does fit a long-standing idea that heavy lifting can help economy, but this paper adds a suggestion rather than a confirmation.

Strength sports

Very high protein and kidney markers first. Bagheri and colleagues in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition randomised forty eight resistance trained men to resistance or concurrent training, for sixteen weeks, on either one point six or three point two grams of protein per kilogram. There were no significant time by protein interactions for any renal marker or eGFR estimate, whichever equation was used. Within the three point two groups, urea nitrogen and cystatin C rose, and eGFR estimates fell. Those are the within-group results a press release would headline, and the authors correctly say they are descriptive because no interaction supports them. Their other caveat matters just as much: with forty four men analysed, the study cannot exclude clinically meaningful differences. Reasonable summary: no signal of harm over four months in healthy young men, which is a long way from proof of safety. One useful detail is that the authors measured cystatin C, which does not depend on muscle mass the way creatinine does, so this rise is harder to dismiss than a creatinine rise in a large lifter. That last point is my own, and it remains a small change without a clinical endpoint.

Next, squats. On Iron Culture this week Eric Trexler and Eric Helms took a question prompted by a viral video of a professional bodybuilder's spinal injury in a Smith machine. The hazard, as they describe it, is a specific one: with a barbell a failed rep can be dumped, while on a Smith machine the load only moves down, so the safety stoppers need setting correctly. They argue it should be seen as a separate machine rather than a poor copy of a barbell squat, and a very quad dominant movement if set up that way. These are coaches' views, and they do not give an injury rate.

A paper from Granada adds something. Marcos-Frutos and colleagues had nineteen strength trained men do four sets of nine at seventy percent of one rep maximum, with free weight and Smith squats in random order. Velocity loss and perceived fatigue did not differ, but twitch interpolation showed more peripheral and central fatigue in the knee extensors after free weights. The authors suggest the Smith squat could be more efficient. Two cautions, in my reading. A single session measuring only the knee extensors says nothing about adaptation, and their premise that adaptations are comparable comes from other work. Also, seventy percent on each implement may be a different relative demand depending on how the one rep maximum was tested, and I cannot see from the abstract how that was handled.

Alyssa Olenick's Hybrid Lab this week is a short and sensible antidote to worry about lifting technique, arguing that form is a skill to develop under loads the lifter can handle, and that most problems come from doing too much too soon. That is coaching opinion. One perspective paper is worth a sentence too. Augustsson, in the journal Sports, cites evidence that only two point three percent of participants in a set of highly cited strength reviews were highly trained or elite. That is a fair warning about reading anything here, including the studies above, as advice for experienced lifters.

Fuelling and protein

A short item, and thin evidence. Callegari and colleagues, also in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition, meta-analysed plant-based versus whey supplementation with resistance training in untrained adults, measured by D E X A. Both groups gained lean mass and lost fat with no significant difference. The soy and pea evidence supports that. Chia rests on a single study that showed no significant change. Five studies in untrained people is not much, and beginners gain muscle under almost any adequate protein arrangement, which I should say is my gloss. It sits alongside the network meta-analysis from episode nine, for anyone on a plant-based diet: pea and soy look like reasonable substitutes for whey in this setting, the abstract gives no pooled effect size, and trained lifters remain largely unstudied.

Heat and hot baths

Keefe and colleagues in the Journal of Applied Physiology studied eighteen highly trained endurance runners, eight men and ten women. They did seven sessions of an hour at fifty percent of velocity at V O two max in forty degrees, and seven in twenty two degrees, in random order. There were no sex differences in the trajectory or size of adaptation in core temperature, heart rate or sweat rate. Heat produced larger falls in core temperature and heart rate and larger rises in sweat rate than temperate running, though temperate running alone produced some adaptation. Plasma volume did not change under either condition. Eight men and ten women is not enough to rule out a modest sex difference, and that is my caution rather than the authors'. Still, it is a useful crossover design in the population that matters, at a controlled work rate, with an interesting absence of plasma volume change.

Next, baths. Zerilli and colleagues at Cardiff Metropolitan, in Experimental Physiology, put sixteen healthy adults, four of them women, through forty five minutes at forty degrees and a time-matched control. Plasma volume fell nine and a half percent immediately after, then returned to baseline within twelve hours with no later expansion. Erythropoietin did not change over twenty four hours. The first author, Owain Zerilli, took the paper through on The Physiology of Endurance Running this week with his co-host and co-author Dan Nash, and it is a good account of how a master's project becomes a paper. They contrast it with a paper from DeMarco and colleagues in Oregon that found an increase at six hours, in women, but without a control condition. Zerilli says the aim of an equal sample was not met, so this one cannot settle the sex question. The abstract's interpretation is that adaptation from repeated bathing may come from sustained changes in plasma volume rather than a pulse of erythropoietin after each bath. A single bout is a narrow test of that, and my own thought is that a serum assay at set time points could miss a brief pulse.

And an open item closed. In episode nine I could report a photobiomodulation study only through Alex Hutchinson's column. I have now found it. Aussieker and colleagues from Luc van Loon's group, in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, treated one leg of twelve people with light and the other with sham, and took biopsies of skin and muscle. Skin warmed by eight degrees. Mitochondrial respiration was not different in either tissue, and of ninety one genes only three differed. That is one session, and tissue measured outside the body. Hutchinson's description holds. The hot tub versus ice bath paper from the same column is still not on PubMed.

A small lab study from Japan is relevant to the shoe side. Sato and colleagues in The Foot had nineteen healthy men who land on their heels run in neutral, rocker and advanced footwear technology shoes. Rocker shoes reduced peak Achilles force and force impulse per step. The advanced shoes did not differ from neutral on any Achilles measure. Those are healthy men at one controlled speed, and the abstract gives no numbers. Nothing here shows that rockers help anyone already injured, but it does tell against the casual assumption that a super shoe relieves the Achilles.

On devices, one white paper is worth reading sceptically, because it is the one place this week that skin tone comes up in wearable accuracy. Temple, a company selling a sensor worn at the temple, published an internal study of forty three people against a Polar H ten chest strap on a treadmill. Temple reports its own mean absolute error at about one point two beats a minute, with Apple Watch Series twelve at one point nine, Fitbit Air at three, WHOOP and Garmin Forerunner nine seventy at a little over three, and Oura Ring five at five point two. It is the manufacturer's own unreviewed study, and the group had nine women out of forty three.
