Health and fitness news

The biggest regulatory item of the week. On the ninth of November the United States Food and Drug Administration, the F D A, required every maker of a G L P one receptor agonist, the drug class that includes semaglutide, to add a warning about non arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy. That is a rare condition that causes sudden, painless loss of vision in one eye, sometimes permanently. The agency's own summary sets out three lines of evidence. A pooled analysis of one hundred and twenty six trials, covering one hundred and thirty four thousand, two hundred and fifty three patients, found ten probable cases in people taking the drugs and five in comparators, an incidence of six point four per hundred thousand person years against three point eight. Second, a study in the Sentinel database of adults with type two diabetes found a hazard ratio of one point four three, with a confidence interval of one point zero two to two point zero one, against D P P four inhibitors, but no significant difference against S G L T two inhibitors, at one point one nine, with an interval crossing one. Third, the agency found no mechanism in the animal data. Put plainly, the absolute risk is small, the trial analysis rests on fifteen events, and the comparison that matters depends on what you compare against. The F D A still judged the signal consistent enough to label.

Next, a California law that the strength world is now discussing. Assembly Bill twenty thirty was signed on the twenty eighth of September. I read the chaptered text this week. It bans selling or giving away an over the counter diet pill, or a dietary supplement for weight loss or muscle building, to anyone under eighteen. Shops must ask for identification where a buyer's age is not clear, and online sellers must verify age against a database or require an adult signature on delivery. The penalty is a civil fine of up to five hundred dollars per violation. The interesting part is how a court decides whether a product counts. The law lists factors a court may consider, and the first is whether the product contains an F D A approved weight loss or muscle building ingredient, a steroid, or, and I quote the list, "Creatine, green tea extract, raspberry ketone, garcinia cambogia, or green coffee bean extract." The other factors are about labelling, marketing, and even where a shop puts the product on the shelf. So creatine is named, but as one factor, not as an automatic ban; a tub of creatine sold with no muscle building claims is a harder case than one shelved next to fat burners. The bill text names no start date. California statutes normally take effect on the first of January after they are signed, so my inference is the first of January, twenty twenty seven. One caution, in my view: creatine monohydrate is one of the best studied supplements in sport, and this law groups it with products that have nothing like that evidence base. That is a policy choice about minors, not a finding about creatine.

Eric Trexler and his co-host Lauren discussed the law on Front Page Fitness, an episode titled Carded For Creatine. On the podcast, one host read the bill's ingredient list and pointed out that the marketing factor, any label implying a product will help maintain or increase muscle or strength, would catch most protein powders as well, which matches my reading of the text. Their broader view, put in my words: teenagers do not need many supplements, but legislating against creatine and whey protein while prescription weight loss drugs and testosterone are a few clicks away online is an odd set of priorities, and it risks feeding the idea that creatine is dangerous. That is commentary from the podcast, not evidence; the evidence point is the one I made above.

A research integrity story inside sports science. Retraction Watch reported on the sixteenth that Biology of Sport, the journal of the Institute of Sport in Warsaw, will correct papers by its own editor in chief, Piotr Zmijewski, that contain references to work that does not exist. He was an author on nine of the one hundred and twenty papers in the latest edition. One paper on Premier League footballers cites two nonexistent works; another, on the ages of male and female footballers, cites sixteen sources that do not exist and four real papers with invented details. Four more of his papers were flagged on PubPeer. Zmijewski told Retraction Watch, "I can confirm that I personally did not use A I in preparing or editing these manuscripts," and blamed an early career researcher who managed a shared reference database. The publisher, Termedia, says it will correct most of the affected articles and has withdrawn one that had not yet appeared in an issue. The practical point for anyone reading the sports science literature is that a reference list is no longer evidence that the cited work exists. This digest runs every reference through PubMed and Crossref before air, and this is why.

Running science

Start with a long running argument about thresholds. Maximal lactate steady state, the highest speed at which blood lactate stays stable, and critical speed, the asymptote of the speed duration curve, are both offered as the boundary between the heavy and severe intensity domains. In running studies they usually disagree, with the lactate steady state coming out slower. A paper in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance, from Ozkaya and colleagues with Mark Burnley and Andrew Jones, proposes that the disagreement comes from how the steady state is defined. The traditional rule is a rise of less than one millimole between ten and thirty minutes. Their individualised version plots the rate of change of lactate against speed across five constant speed runs and finds a breakpoint. In eight endurance runners, the traditional method gave thirteen point two kilometres an hour, the individualised method fourteen point zero, and critical speed fourteen point zero. The two agreed almost exactly, with a bias of minus zero point zero two kilometres an hour and limits of agreement from minus zero point one five to plus zero point one.

Three cautions. Eight runners is a very small sample, and a correlation reported as one point zero in eight people should be read with caution. The method needs five long runs plus four or more runs to exhaustion, which is a lab protocol, not a field test. And the authors are, in effect, redefining the steady state until it matches critical speed, so a sceptic would say the match is partly built in. The strongest version of their case, as I read it, is that the one millimole rule is a convention, and that a breakpoint in how fast lactate rises sits closer to the physiology. My inference, for coaches who control threshold work by heart rate and perceived effort rather than by lactate: this paper does not change where the ceiling is, but it is a reason to treat a lactate steady state from the traditional rule as a conservative estimate rather than the boundary itself.

Next, ageing. A systematic review and meta analysis in Sports Medicine, from Shalaya Kipp and colleagues at the Mayo Clinic, pooled sixty nine studies of directly measured V O two max in one thousand, nine hundred and three female masters endurance athletes aged forty and over. Pooled values were forty five millilitres per kilogram per minute in the forties, forty in the fifties, thirty five in the sixties and thirty above seventy. Each extra year of age was associated with zero point five seven millilitres per kilogram per minute lower V O two max, about eleven point seven percent a decade, and that held across running, cycling and swimming. The limitation is in the design: these are cross sectional differences between age groups, not the same women followed over time, and athletes who stay competitive into their seventies are a selected group. Still, the authors present these as female specific reference values, filling a gap in a literature where the decline is well documented mainly in men.

A smaller study in the Journal of Experimental Biology, from Lemineur and colleagues in Nice, asks why older runners also become less economical. Eighteen young and eighteen master athletes ran on a treadmill at two and a half and three metres per second. The masters' net cost of running was higher, four point zero two against three point eight one joules per kilogram per metre at the slower speed, and four point two nine against three point eight one at the faster one. They also did less work at the ankle and more at the hip, the familiar shift from distal to proximal. But here is the honest part: joint work did not correlate with cost of transport, and the authors say whether these changes cause the higher cost "remains to be established." The tidy story, weaker calves therefore worse economy, was not supported by their own data.

Now altitude. Alex Hutchinson's Outside column on the twelfth covered a paper in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance by Atle and Simen Guttormsen, of the School of Economics and Business at the Norwegian University of Life Sciences. They took six hundred and eighty eight thousand running results from N C A A indoor meets at three hundred and eighty two venues, and used athlete by season fixed effects, so each performance is compared with that athlete's other races that season, with a separate term for banked tracks. Sprints were faster at altitude, by zero point three to zero point five percent per thousand metres. Distance events were slower, with the penalty rising from zero point five to zero point nine percent per thousand metres at eight hundred metres to two point seven to three percent at five thousand. The crossover between the drag benefit and the oxygen cost fell between four hundred and eight hundred metres. Women's eight hundred metre times were penalised nearly twice as much as men's, zero point nine two against zero point five percent, which Hutchinson explains by the women running longer and slower, so they gain less from lower drag and pay more for less oxygen. Hutchinson works this out to roughly thirty seconds per thousand metres of altitude over five thousand metres, and reports that the N C A A scrapped its altitude conversions over the summer. These are collegiate athletes indoors, with no measure of acclimatisation, so my suggestion is to treat the numbers as a population average, not a personal correction. Hutchinson himself notes that the right correction differs between people.

A second altitude paper adds sex to the picture, in the lab. In the Journal of Physiology, Hansen and colleagues in Odense and Oslo had twenty two trained cyclists and triathletes, ten of them women, ride thirty minute time trials at about four hundred metres and at a simulated two thousand eight hundred metres, with arterial and femoral venous sampling and muscle biopsies. Performance dropped in hypoxia by fourteen percent in women and eighteen percent in men. Women rode at a higher fraction of their V O two max at both altitudes and drew relatively more energy from blood glucose. Men showed a large fall in whole body carbohydrate oxidation in hypoxia; women a modest one. At the leg, both sexes responded similarly: less glycogen oxidised, no change in fat or blood glucose use. The authors flag their fat estimates as uncertain. Put next to the track data, and this is my reading, there are now two independent hints that women and men do not lose the same amount at altitude, though in opposite directions for different reasons, which is a reason not to over read either.

One more, on prescribing intervals by feel, with Carl Foster as senior author, the researcher behind session R P E. In the European Journal of Sport Science, Bok and colleagues had thirteen male physical education students run three three minute intervals either at R P E five on the Borg category ratio scale, or at eighty five percent of maximal aerobic speed. Time spent above ninety percent of V O two max did not differ. The R P E session produced lower peak heart rate, one hundred and eighty three point seven against one hundred and eighty seven point nine, lower lactate, nine point six against eleven point seven millimoles, and lower session R P E. The title calls this the same gain for less pain. The caveats are real: these are not trained runners, it is one session, and several of the p values sit just under zero point zero five. It is consistent with the case for perceived effort as a prescription tool, not proof of it.

Heat and fuelling

The fuelling study of the week, and Brady Holmer covered it in his Physiology Friday on the thirteenth. In the Journal of Physiology, Tim Podlogar, Gareth Wallis and colleagues at Birmingham and Exeter, with the United States Army Research Institute of Environmental Medicine, asked whether heat acclimation restores the body's ability to burn ingested carbohydrate in the heat. Seventeen endurance trained adults, ten men and seven women, cycled for ninety minutes at forty five percent of peak power while drinking glucose at ninety grams an hour, first at twenty degrees, then at forty degrees, both at twenty percent humidity, and then again at forty degrees after nine days of heat acclimation over ten days. Dual stable isotope tracers and muscle biopsies tracked where the glucose went.

Exogenous glucose oxidation, the ingested sugar actually burned, was about twenty seven percent lower in the heat, zero point three seven grams a minute against zero point five one in the cool. After acclimation it was zero point three eight, still suppressed, even though heart rate, core temperature and perceived strain all improved. The rate at which the ingested glucose appeared in the blood did not change. What fell was clearance: the tissues took up and burned less of it. The authors conclude that the limit is in peripheral glucose disposal, not in the gut.

Holmer's reading is careful. He says not to count on acclimation to restore carbohydrate oxidation, but to acclimate anyway for the cardiovascular and thermal benefits. On whether to add thirty percent more carbohydrate in the heat to compensate, his answer is "Probably not," because there is no evidence the extra would be used. He also suggests gut training improves carbohydrate oxidation in general, which is a separate claim the study does not test. The limitations: this is cycling at a moderate, steady intensity after an overnight fast, in dry heat, at one dose. Race intensity is higher, and real races are often humid. Episode nine covered a related study on cold fluids and glucose oxidation in the heat; this one explains more of the mechanism.

A review in Sports Medicine from Julien Périard's group in Canberra looked at what elite athletes actually do to prepare for hot competitions. The laboratory guidelines say ten to fourteen consecutive days of heat exposure, sixty to ninety minutes a day, starting at least two weeks out. Across twelve observational studies of elite and world class athletes, eleven used sport specific heat exposure, often in conditions as hot as or hotter than competition, and five added passive heating after exercise. The authors' main complaint is reporting: protocols and monitoring varied so much, and were described so thinly, that it is hard to judge which ones worked. The usual markers of adaptation, lower exercise heart rate and core temperature and a larger plasma volume, were commonly seen.

Now a small dose of carbohydrate and durability. In the Scandinavian Journal of Medicine and Science in Sports, Iten and colleagues, with Plews and Maunder in Auckland, had thirteen trained cyclists and triathletes ride for two and a half hours at ninety percent of their first ventilatory threshold, with either fifteen grams of carbohydrate an hour or a placebo, and then measured their intensity domain boundaries. Fresh, power at the first threshold was one hundred and ninety three watts. After the long ride with placebo it was one hundred and seventy six; with fifteen grams an hour, one hundred and eighty nine, no different from fresh. Critical power was three hundred and twelve watts fresh against two hundred and ninety eight with placebo, and with fifteen grams an hour it was no different from placebo. So a very low intake protected the lower boundary, the one most relevant to long steady work, with no evidence it protected the upper one. Thirteen riders, and the blood glucose comparisons are labelled exploratory. The practical reading, which is mine: for long sessions done deliberately low in carbohydrate, even a modest intake may preserve the intensity at which easy running stays easy.

Louise Burke appeared on That Triathlon Show this week answering listener questions on Ironman carbohydrate intake. The public feed carries only the opening few minutes, so I will report only what is in that. Asked whether the target should be one hundred and twenty or one hundred and fifty grams an hour, she said, on the podcast, that "most of what we know at the moment is being driven by athlete behavior watching what people are doing in the field rather than having great studies to direct us," and that the answer depends on who is racing: a winner and someone chasing the seventeen hour cut off burn fuel at very different rates. That is a useful frame for the Podlogar result. Very high intake targets come largely from field practice, and a lab study now says that in the heat, less of what is drunk gets burned.

One more fuelling item, a preprint, so not yet peer reviewed. On SportRxiv on the seventeenth, James Steele, Stuart Phillips and colleagues posted an updated systematic review and Bayesian dose response meta analysis of protein intake and changes in lean mass, muscle size, strength and physical function in healthy adults. They included ninety five trials with usable data, four thousand, nine hundred and thirty one participants, and rather than assuming a straight line or a fixed breakpoint, they averaged across twenty six possible shapes for the dose response. The associations were modestly positive, especially with resistance training. But no supported contrast between a current and a proposed intake produced a conclusively meaningful gain or loss; changes of about zero point two to zero point five grams per kilogram per day from a current intake were generally practically equivalent; and in the authors' words, "We could not identify a clearly optimal intake." Most trials had some concerns or a high risk of bias, adjusting for publication bias pulled the estimates towards zero, and certainty was low or very low throughout. That is a sobering read for anyone who treats a precise daily protein target as settled science.

Last in this segment, bone density and plant based diets, because NutritionFacts dot org published a piece on it this week, and Michael Greger's claim is specific enough to check. He argues that the lower bone density seen in vegetarians and vegans is mostly explained by their smaller body size. The key study is Karavasiloglou and colleagues, in the Journal of Nutrition in twenty twenty, using the United States N H A N E S survey. After adjusting for body mass index and waist circumference, the difference at the femoral neck and the whole femur was no longer significant, zero point seven seven against zero point seven nine grams per square centimetre, and zero point eight eight against zero point nine. But at the lumbar spine a small difference remained and was statistically significant, one point zero one against one point zero four. Earlier meta analyses found bone density about four percent lower in vegetarians, and six percent lower at the spine in vegans, in a twenty oh nine Bayesian analysis of nine studies; and a twenty nineteen review of twenty studies and thirty seven thousand participants found higher fracture rates in vegans. Greger's case that body size explains the gap is an interpretation of observational data, not a tested result. A fair summary for anyone on a plant based diet: body size explains part of the bone density gap, not all of it, and the spine difference survives adjustment.

Hybrid racing

There was no new trial on concurrent training this week that I could find, and I would rather say so than pad the segment. What there was is a case study in hybrid racing from an Olympian. Colleen Quigley, eighth in the steeplechase in Rio, entered her first Hyrox in Denver in the open division and won it in one hour, eight minutes and three seconds, more than two minutes clear of a field of over fifteen hundred women, according to Marathon Handbook. A Hyrox is eight one kilometre runs, each followed by a workout station. Her running totalled thirty two minutes and eighteen seconds, an average of four minutes and two seconds per kilometre, the fastest of any open woman. On the stations, she ranked sixteenth. She also did six sled pulls instead of four after getting turned around.

The pro women raced the next day and Rachael Wade won in one hour and fifty seconds. Quigley's time would have placed ninth, but the pro sled is one hundred and fifty two kilograms against one hundred and two, and the wall ball six kilograms against four, so the comparison does not hold. What the split shows, and this is my reading, is the shape of the sport: a world class runner can win an open race on the running alone while being ordinary on the stations, which is exactly the gap the strength side of hybrid training is meant to close.

RED S on Fast Talk

Fast Talk episode four hundred and forty nine is an hour on relative energy deficiency in sport with Trent Stellingwerff and Kate Ackerman, hosted by Chris Case and Julie Young. Both contributed to the International Olympic Committee's RED S clinical assessment tool, version two. I transcribed the episode locally, and this is a machine transcript, so I will paraphrase and attribute it to the podcast rather than to one speaker.

The core definition, as described on the podcast: RED S is a syndrome of impaired physiological or psychological function in female and male athletes, caused by exposure to problematic low energy availability, meaning prolonged or severe. The word problematic matters. The guests distinguish it from adaptable low energy availability, the short term deficit of a hard day or a deliberate weight loss phase, which does not by itself cause the syndrome. They also stress that diagnosis works from the outcomes, the health and performance consequences, rather than from trying to measure energy availability directly.

Several practical points came through. Bone stress injuries are central: several in the last two or three years is a flag. In men, changes in libido and morning erections can be an early sign, the male counterpart to menstrual disruption. Different symptoms carry different weight and appear on different timelines. And early detection changes the outlook: an adolescent corrected at fifteen or sixteen does far better than a woman presenting in her twenties after years of deficiency, who carries a higher risk of future bone injury. One guest mentioned working with a young vegetarian man with low bone density, and a past paper on links between specific diets and low energy availability; I have not read that paper, so I will not characterise it.

This is a clinical consensus view from two of its contributors, not a new trial. A scoping review this autumn in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living found thirty two studies using the terms and screening tools inconsistently, with only one using the assessment tool the guests helped write, and awareness of RED S ranging from ten to ninety two percent across samples, which fits the guests' point that practice has not caught up with the definition.

Strength sports

Three papers. First, how well people judge proximity to failure. In the Journal of Sports Sciences, Holmes and colleagues, with Eric Helms, had thirty two people, nineteen trained and thirteen untrained, do machine chest press and leg extension at seventy five percent of one repetition maximum. In one session they called out when they reached two reps in reserve, in the other when they reached an eight on the Borg R P E scale, then kept going to failure. Accuracy was the same either way: the mean difference was minus zero point zero three reps for chest press, with a confidence interval from minus zero point five to plus zero point four four, and zero point one six for leg extension. Training status made no difference. This is machines and one load, and the paper only says the two scales are equally accurate, not that either is accurate in absolute terms. But it undercuts the idea that one scale is clearly better for prescribing effort.

Second, how fast to add volume. In the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, Enes and colleagues in Brazil used a within subject design in twenty one recreationally trained women: one leg added two weekly sets for the quadriceps every two weeks, the other every four weeks, for twelve weeks. Both legs got stronger and bigger, with no detectable difference between them in leg press strength or muscle thickness. Twenty one people, and the authors limit their conclusion to the relatively high volumes they tested. It is a null result about the rate of progression, not evidence that progression does not matter.

Third, a narrative review in Sports Medicine from Graz on supramaximal accentuated eccentric loading, where the lowering phase uses more than the concentric one rep max and the lifting phase less. The authors report meaningful strength gains with as little as two to four sets and three to eight eccentric heavy reps a week, though they suggest trained lifters may need five or more sets. They are frank that direct comparisons with ordinary training are sparse, interventions short and methods varied. It is a hypothesis about time efficiency, not a recommendation.

One supplement paper sits here. In Biological Research for Nursing, Oliveira and colleagues, including Candow and Forbes, randomised forty eight women, twenty six young and twenty two older, to three grams of creatine monohydrate a day or placebo for twenty eight days. The creatine groups improved on several cognitive measures, most consistently reaction time, inhibitory control and working memory, with the strongest Bayesian evidence in the older women. The authors call their test battery exploratory and multi domain and say the result needs confirming in hypothesis driven trials. Many outcomes, small groups, and baseline creatine intake differed between groups. Interesting, not settled.

Gear and technology

Start with a study this digest watches for: whether wearable sensors work equally across skin tones. In Frontiers in Digital Health, Miller and colleagues at Lehigh University compared four consumer wearable pulse oximeters with two clinical monitors in forty nine adults, at rest, walking on a treadmill and in recovery. Skin tone was measured objectively with the individual typology angle as well as the Fitzpatrick scale. Agreement varied by device and activity: the Garmin read about zero point seven two percentage points low during recovery, and the Wellue about one point zero three low on the treadmill. After correcting for multiple comparisons, no device showed a significant association between skin pigmentation and error. Just as notable, the Garmin produced readings only eighty two point four percent of the time on the treadmill, and the Apple Watch zero point four percent. Two cautions. Forty nine people is a small sample for detecting a skin tone effect, so a null result here is not proof of no bias. And this is walking, not running. In my view the method, an objective colour measure rather than a visual category, is the right one, and in this sample activity and data dropout mattered more than pigmentation.
